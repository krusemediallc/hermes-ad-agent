# Fifteen reusable direct-response frameworks

These are strategic skeletons, not scripts. Pick the framework that fits the
creative, audience awareness, and verified receipts. Replace every bracketed
field with a real fact or remove the line.

When producing a variant set, use different frameworks and proof points. A new
framework wrapped around the same unsupported claim is not a new angle.

## Quick picker

| Available material | Framework |
|---|---|
| A real operator moment or build story | 1. First-person operator story |
| A concrete feature set with mechanisms | 2. Feature-to-benefit proof stack |
| A verified customer quote | 3. Verbatim testimonial |
| A verified customer story in their words | 4. First-person customer account |
| A real deadline or capacity limit | 5. Real urgency |
| One strong, sourced number | 6. Statistic lead |
| Three unusually specific reasons | 7. Three reasons |
| Measured old and new states | 8. Before-and-after process |
| A sharply observed audience pain | 9. Relatable moment |
| A visual that carries the pitch | 10. Teaser or visual pun |
| Evidence against a common belief | 11. Challenge the status quo |
| A real origin story | 12. Founder story |
| A genuine discovery moment | 13. Peer discovery |
| A strong line already in the creative | 14. Creative-aligned hook |
| A precise qualification condition | 15. If-then qualifier |

## 1. First-person operator story

**Use when:** A founder, practitioner, or team member has a real moment that
shows why the offer matters.

**Evidence needed:** What happened, when, what was tried, and the observable
result.

**Skeleton:**

1. Open with `I` and the specific moment.
2. Describe the action in plain chronological order.
3. Give the measured outcome or useful lesson.
4. Connect the reader to the offer.
5. Make one low-friction ask.

**Watchout:** Do not force a perfect hero arc. Keep relevant friction if it
actually happened.

**Template:**

> I spent [verified time] on [specific task] last [day or period]. [Concrete
> failure or observation]. We built [mechanism] to handle [exact step]. In
> [measured test], it [verified result]. [CTA].

## 2. Feature-to-benefit proof stack

**Use when:** The offer has several features that solve distinct jobs and a cold
reader needs to understand what is included.

**Evidence needed:** Exact features, how each works, and any supported outcome.

**Skeleton:**

1. Frame the job the product handles.
2. List each feature with its mechanism or consequence.
3. Add a proof point or boundary.
4. State price or terms when useful.
5. Give the CTA.

**Watchout:** Do not pad the list to a neat number. Avoid checkmark or emoji
scaffolding and grammatically identical bullets.

**Template:**

> [Product] handles [specific job]:
>
> - [Feature] [does concrete action].
> - [Feature] uses [mechanism], so [supported benefit].
> - [Longer or shorter item with a real constraint].
> - [Another included item, if it matters].
>
> [Verified proof or terms]. [CTA].

## 3. Verbatim testimonial

**Use when:** A real customer quote is stronger than brand narration.

**Evidence needed:** Original quote, permission, accurate attribution, and
context for any result claim.

**Skeleton:**

1. Use the customer's exact words.
2. Attribute according to the approved privacy level.
3. Add brief context only when needed to understand the quote.

**Watchout:** Never create, combine, polish, or "reconstruct" a testimonial.
If no approved quote exists, choose another framework.

**Template:**

> "[Approved quote copied verbatim.]"
>
> [Approved attribution], [relevant context]

## 4. First-person customer account

**Use when:** A customer has given permission to tell a longer before, action,
and result story in their own words.

**Evidence needed:** Source interview, post, email, or recording, plus permission
and support for all results.

**Skeleton:**

1. Customer describes the old situation.
2. Customer describes what they chose and did.
3. Customer gives the specific result and timeframe.
4. Attribution closes the account.

**Watchout:** This is still testimonial copy. Do not ghostwrite thoughts the
customer never expressed.

**Template:**

> "[What I was doing before, in the customer's words]. [Why I tried the offer].
> [What changed, including the verified measure and timeframe]."
>
> [Approved attribution]

## 5. Real urgency

**Use when:** A deadline, inventory count, event date, cohort close, or capacity
limit is factual and consequential.

**Evidence needed:** Exact deadline or constraint, timezone where relevant, and
what objectively changes afterward.

**Skeleton:**

1. State the deadline or remaining capacity plainly.
2. Explain what ends or changes.
3. Remind the reader of the relevant value.
4. Give one time-matched action.

**Watchout:** Never invent scarcity, extend a "final" deadline repeatedly, or
use countdown language for an evergreen offer.

**Template:**

> Registration closes [date, time, timezone]. After that, [specific consequence].
> The [event or offer] includes [relevant verified detail]. [CTA before deadline].

## 6. Statistic lead

**Use when:** One sourced number reframes the problem or makes the outcome
legible.

**Evidence needed:** Number, definition, timeframe, sample or denominator when
material, and source.

**Skeleton:**

1. Lead with the number in context.
2. Explain what it means for this reader.
3. Name the mechanism or next action.
4. Cite or qualify the source where needed.

**Watchout:** Precision does not equal truth. Avoid orphan percentages, cherry
picked windows, and private benchmark claims presented as universal.

**Template:**

> [Verified number] across [defined sample and period]. The gap came from
> [supported mechanism], not a guess. [What the reader can inspect or do]. [CTA].

## 7. Three reasons

**Use when:** Exactly three concrete reasons make the choice easier to evaluate.
Use the number intentionally, not because three sounds complete.

**Evidence needed:** Three distinct reasons, each with a fact or mechanism.

**Skeleton:**

1. Frame why these three reasons matter.
2. Give reason one with proof.
3. Give reason two in a different sentence shape.
4. Let reason three vary in length or format.
5. Close without restating all three.

**Watchout:** A perfect triad of vague benefits reads manufactured. If there are
two strong reasons, publish two. If there are four, do not hide one.

**Template:**

> Three reasons [audience] chose [offer] in [period]:
>
> 1. [Reason plus proof].
> 2. [Reason explained through mechanism].
> 3. [Specific detail in a deliberately different rhythm].
>
> [CTA].

## 8. Before-and-after process

**Use when:** The offer changes a workflow, cost, delay, error rate, or other
observable state.

**Evidence needed:** Comparable old and new measures, same scope, timeframe,
and relevant tradeoffs.

**Skeleton:**

1. Show the old state with measured steps.
2. Name the intervention.
3. Show the new state using the same measure.
4. Disclose a meaningful constraint.
5. Give the CTA.

**Watchout:** Do not imply causation from unrelated snapshots. Avoid miraculous
overnight timelines and compressed customer stories.

**Template:**

> Before: [old steps and total].
>
> We changed [specific mechanism].
>
> After [timeframe]: [new steps and comparable total].
>
> [Constraint or scope]. [CTA].

## 9. Relatable moment

**Use when:** A precise observation can make the intended reader feel understood
before the offer appears.

**Evidence needed:** Real customer language, support themes, search behavior, or
repeated operator experience.

**Skeleton:**

1. Name the exact situation.
2. Add the detail that makes it recognizable.
3. Explain the underlying cause without blaming the reader.
4. Introduce the relevant mechanism.
5. Give the CTA.

**Watchout:** "Tired of struggling?" could address anyone. Use details that
exclude people outside the audience.

**Template:**

> You have [specific setup] open and still need to [frustrating manual step]
> before [real deadline]. [Observed reason this happens]. [Product] handles
> [specific part]. [CTA].

## 10. Teaser or visual pun

**Use when:** The image or video communicates most of the proposition and one
short line can create curiosity or sharpen the joke.

**Evidence needed:** Strong alignment between the line, visual, and landing page.

**Skeleton:**

1. Write one line, usually 5 to 15 words.
2. Let the creative complete the meaning.

**Watchout:** If the line needs an explanation, it is not doing its job. Do not
let a clever phrase obscure the product or action.

**Template:**

> [Short line that names or twists the visual's central detail.]

## 11. Challenge the status quo

**Use when:** The audience holds a common belief that the available evidence can
fairly challenge.

**Evidence needed:** The actual belief, evidence against it, and a credible
alternative mechanism.

**Skeleton:**

1. Name the common practice without exaggerating consensus.
2. Show where it breaks with an example or data.
3. State the better mechanism directly.
4. Connect the offer to that mechanism.
5. Give a low-pressure CTA.

**Watchout:** Avoid the `not X, but Y` rhetorical seesaw. Do not invent an enemy
or dismiss a practice that works in contexts outside the claim.

**Template:**

> [Common practice] breaks when [specific condition]. In [verified example],
> [evidence]. [Alternative mechanism] handles that by [how]. [Offer connection].
> [CTA].

## 12. Founder story

**Use when:** The origin of the company explains a product decision, mission, or
tradeoff that matters to buyers.

**Evidence needed:** Real chronology, founder motivation, build decisions, and
current state.

**Skeleton:**

1. Start at the concrete frustration or observation.
2. Show what the founder tried.
3. Explain the product decision that followed.
4. State where the product is now with honest scale.
5. Invite the reader into the next step.

**Watchout:** Keep the story grounded. Avoid destiny language, category-changing
claims, and a mission statement that could belong to any company.

**Template:**

> In [date or period], I kept running into [specific problem]. I tried [attempt],
> and [what failed]. That led us to build [product decision]. Today, [verified
> current state]. [CTA].

## 13. Peer discovery

**Use when:** A real recommendation, group conversation, search, or social post
led someone to discover the offer.

**Evidence needed:** Genuine discovery channel, reaction, test, and result.

**Skeleton:**

1. Say where the person found it.
2. Give the honest initial reaction.
3. Describe what they checked or tried.
4. State the supported result.
5. Offer the same next step.

**Watchout:** Do not fake virality, trends, or word of mouth. Match the discovery
channel to the actual audience.

**Template:**

> [Person or role] sent me [specific thing] in [real channel]. I expected
> [honest concern], so I tested [what]. [Verified observation]. [CTA].

## 14. Creative-aligned hook

**Use when:** The opening line, demonstration, or visual contrast in the creative
already carries the strongest hook.

**Evidence needed:** Final creative transcript or storyboard and matching landing
page context.

**Skeleton:**

1. Lift or closely echo the creative's strongest line.
2. Add context only if the placement needs it.
3. Keep the headline and CTA consistent with the creative.

**Watchout:** Do not promise a result in text that the creative only depicts as
an illustration. Avoid competing hooks.

**Template:**

> [Verbatim or faithful opening line from the creative]. [Optional proof or CTA].

## 15. If-then qualifier

**Use when:** A concrete condition can qualify the right buyer and disqualify the
wrong one.

**Evidence needed:** Specific situation, supported diagnosis, and a relevant
next step.

**Skeleton:**

1. Use one precise `if` condition.
2. State the implication or action.
3. Explain the mechanism briefly.
4. Give the CTA.

**Watchout:** Avoid stacked `if` clauses, generic aspirations, and negative
parallelism after the condition.

**Template:**

> If [specific observable situation], then check [specific measure or action].
> [Offer] helps by [mechanism]. [CTA].

## Framework quality gate

Before shipping, confirm:

- The chosen framework fits the evidence rather than hiding its absence.
- Every bracket has been replaced or the line has been removed.
- Testimonial, urgency, and statistic frameworks have source records.
- The hook agrees with the creative and destination.
- Variants differ in angle, proof, and framework, not just wording.
